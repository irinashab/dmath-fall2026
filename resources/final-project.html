Final project

The final project is a team presentation and a project paper on a focused question related to discrete mathematics. Your task is to teach your classmates something beyond our regular coursework while using the definitions, examples, and proof methods we have studied.

Build your project around one focused question, one substantial worked example, and one mathematical argument. Original research is not expected. Understanding and clearly explaining existing mathematics is the goal.

Teams and participation

Teams will normally have three students. With the current enrollment of 19 students, we will have five teams of three and two teams of two, for seven teams altogether.

Every member should make a substantive contribution and understand the central definitions, example, and argument. Divide the work, but review the complete project together so that you can explain how the parts fit together.

Two-person teams have the same learning goals and presentation time; a somewhat narrower topic scope can be agreed upon during proposal review.

Initial proposal

Post your team’s initial proposal by Thursday, October 1. Include:

  1. The names of all team members.
  2. A tentative title and a focused mathematical question.
  3. A brief explanation of how the question connects to this course and why it interests your team.
  4. The example you plan to develop and the proof, algorithm, or mathematical argument you expect to explain.
  5. One or two preliminary sources.

The proposal is a starting plan, not a completed paper. You do not need to have finished the proof yet, but identify what you intend to investigate. We will use the proposal to check that the scope is realistic and refine it where necessary.

What the mathematics should include

Every project should introduce the definitions needed for its question, develop at least one substantial worked example, and explain at least one proof or justified mathematical argument that the team understands.

  • A theorem project might give a proof and illustrate the theorem with a concrete example.
  • An algorithm project might explain why the algorithm works or justify a running-time bound.
  • An application project should explain the mathematics behind the application, not merely describe where it is used.
  • For an advanced topic, you may state a major theorem without proving it, provided you clearly say so and give an accessible supporting argument or result in detail.

Choose a scope that lets you explain something carefully. A broad survey of an entire subject is usually less effective than a well-developed answer to one question.

The presentation

Each team has 12 minutes total: 10 minutes for the presentation and 2 minutes for questions. Rehearse to finish within the 10-minute speaking limit. Seven teams use 84 minutes, leaving only a short amount of time for transitions.

Present for classmates who know the course material but have not studied your topic. Give enough context to explain why the question matters, then use a clear example to introduce the main mathematical idea. Explain the structure of your argument rather than rushing through every technical detail.

A possible organization for the 10-minute presentation is:

  • 2 minutes: the question, motivation, and essential definitions.
  • 3 minutes: a substantial worked example.
  • 4 minutes: the central proof idea, algorithm, or mathematical argument.
  • 1 minute: what the result tells us and its connection to the course.

This is a suggested structure, not a required script. All team members should be prepared to participate in explaining the project and answering questions.

Project paper

Your team’s final paper is due Monday, December 7. Write a clear explanation of your topic for a classmate who has taken this course. Introduce the necessary ideas, work through an example, and explain your mathematical argument. The paper should extend the presentation by explaining the mathematics in greater detail.

A useful organization is:

  1. Question and motivation. State the problem and explain its connection to discrete mathematics.
  2. Definitions and background. Introduce the notation and assumptions the reader needs.
  3. Worked example. Walk through a concrete case, explaining the steps.
  4. Mathematical argument. Present a proof, an algorithm with justification, or another substantial argument. Distinguish results you prove from results you cite.
  5. Discussion. Explain what the argument establishes, why its assumptions matter, and any relevant limitations.
  6. References. Give enough information for the reader to locate your sources.

Use clear mathematical prose. Sentences such as “We now show…” are appropriate. Define symbols, explain transitions, and check that examples satisfy the hypotheses of the results you use.

Cite sources where you use their results, proofs, explanations, or figures, and include a reference list. You may explain an existing proof in your own words, with attribution. A bibliography alone does not identify which claims came from which source.

Possible topics

The following list is a starting point, not an exhaustive menu. You may propose another topic related to discrete mathematics. Each bullet is a direction to narrow into a question, not a requirement to cover everything mentioned. Topics marked advanced need particularly careful scope review.

Logic

  • Non-classical logic: choose one many-valued, modal, relevance, or intuitionistic logic. Introduce its rules and compare a specific example with classical logic.
  • Satisfiability: explain a small SAT-solving method and trace it on a propositional formula. Distinguish finding a satisfying assignment from showing that none exists.
  • Gödel’s incompleteness theorems — advanced: carefully state one theorem, explain its assumptions, and develop an accessible supporting idea. A proof of the full theorem is not expected.
  • Undecidability of arithmetic truth — advanced: investigate the claim that no algorithm decides the truth of every first-order sentence about the natural numbers with addition and multiplication. Distinguish this question from incompleteness of a formal proof system.

Set theory

  • Russell’s paradox: explain the contradiction and how restricting set formation avoids the unrestricted construction.
  • Cantor’s theorem: prove that no function from a set onto its power set exists, and explain the consequence for comparing sizes.
  • Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein theorem: investigate why injections in both directions imply a bijection.
  • The axiom of choice — advanced: select one formulation or consequence and explain it through examples.
  • Ordinal or cardinal arithmetic — advanced: compare a few concrete operations rather than attempting a general survey.
  • Representing mathematical objects by sets: explain one construction, such as ordered pairs, natural numbers, or the integers from the natural numbers.

Counting and discrete probability

  • The pigeonhole principle: develop a substantial application and explain precisely what the objects and categories are.
  • Ramsey theory: prove that among six people there must be three mutual acquaintances or three mutual strangers, assuming acquaintance is symmetric.
  • Constrained counting: count arrangements with repeated symbols or specified restrictions and justify the method.
  • Integer partitions: investigate a particular identity or correspondence between classes of partitions.
  • Discrete probability: choose a finite probability problem, justify the sample space, and explain the counting behind the calculation.

Algorithms, recurrences, and growth

  • Recursive algorithms: analyze binary search, mergesort, quicksort, or a recursive Fibonacci algorithm. State whether you study worst-case, average-case, or another running time.
  • Growth classes: show that \(\Theta\) is an equivalence relation on eventually positive functions and that \(O\) induces a partial order on the resulting classes. Give functions whose growth classes are incomparable.
  • Limit superior — advanced: explain motivating examples and investigate how a bound on \(\limsup f(n)/g(n)\) characterizes \(f=O(g)\) for eventually positive functions.
  • Graph-algorithm analysis: select one algorithm and explain how the representation of its input affects its running time.

Number theory and binary representations

Featured option: the Chinese Remainder Theorem through the pigeonhole principle. For relatively prime positive integers \(m\) and \(n\), prove that each prescribed pair of residues determines a unique residue modulo \(mn\):

\[x\equiv a\pmod m,\qquad x\equiv b\pmod n.\]

Include a worked example and explain where relative primality is used. One route is to examine the residues modulo \(n\) of

\[a,\ a+m,\ a+2m,\ldots,a+(n-1)m.\]

Why can no two have the same residue? Why must every residue occur? Identify the use of the pigeonhole principle, and separately justify uniqueness modulo \(mn\).

  • Fermat’s Little Theorem: explain a proof and an application.
  • Euler’s totient function: investigate invertible residues and compare prime and composite moduli.
  • Modular algorithms: explain repeated squaring for modular exponentiation and justify its efficiency.
  • Cryptography: develop a small example and explain the number-theoretic argument that makes it work.
  • Divisibility as a lattice: use the positive integers ordered by divisibility; show how gcd and lcm give greatest lower bounds and least upper bounds.
  • Signed binary integers: compare ones’ complement and two’s complement, including a worked arithmetic example.
  • Binary floating-point: explain representation and rounding in a small illustrative format.
  • Gray codes: construct an ordering of binary strings in which consecutive strings differ in one bit, and prove the construction works.
  • Calendars, magic squares, or Pythagorean triples: choose a precise mathematical question and develop its structure, not only its history.

Relations and orderings

  • Lattices and Boolean algebras: develop one concrete example and prove its defining properties.
  • Preorders: investigate reflexive, transitive relations and how identifying mutually related elements can produce a partial order.
  • Relations that are not equivalence relations: construct meaningful examples and identify exactly which properties fail.

Graphs and trees

  • Graph algorithms: choose shortest paths, minimum spanning trees, topological sorting, or maximum flow and minimum cut. Explain the assumptions and a correctness argument.
  • Prüfer codes: explain a correspondence between labeled trees and sequences, with a worked encoding and decoding example.
  • Planarity: investigate a particular obstruction to a planar drawing, or an embedding on another surface.
  • Graph representations: compare adjacency matrices and adjacency lists through a specific task, such as testing connectivity.
  • Network centrality: compare two measures on a small network and explain what each captures.
  • Computational complexity — advanced: select a precisely stated decision problem, such as vertex cover with a size bound, clique with the requested size as part of the input, 3-colorability, Hamiltonian cycle, or traveling salesperson with a cost bound. Explain how a proposed solution can be checked, and distinguish a decision problem from its optimization version. Proving NP-completeness requires more than showing membership in NP.
  • Infinite binary trees: explain why the set of finite nodes is countable while the set of infinite branches is uncountable.
  • Infinite graphs or order automorphisms — advanced: choose one structure, such as the natural numbers or integers with their usual order, and characterize its order-preserving symmetries.

Before submitting

  • Does the project answer a focused question?
  • Are the necessary definitions and assumptions stated?
  • Is there a substantial example and a justified mathematical argument?
  • Can every team member explain the central idea?
  • Are borrowed results and materials attributed?
  • Has the presentation been rehearsed within 10 minutes?
