1. Directed and weighted graphs

Last time we used undirected edges: AB and BA describe the same connection. Some situations require more information.

Directed graphs

A directed graph (digraph) has edges called arcs, with a specified source and target. An arc (u,v), written u → v, need not be accompanied by v → u. For a simple loopless digraph, the arc set is a subset of {(u,v) ∈ V × V : u ≠ v}.

Examples include one-way streets and links between web pages. A directed walk must follow each arrow. The out-degree deg⁺(v) counts arcs leaving v; the in-degree deg⁻(v) counts arcs entering v. A directed loop, if allowed, contributes one to each.

Try: On vertices P, Q, R, take arcs P → Q, Q → R, R → P, P → R. List the in-degree and out-degree of each vertex. Is P → R → Q a directed walk?
Show answer

P: in 1, out 2. Q: in 1, out 1. R: in 2, out 1. The proposed walk fails because R → Q is absent. Each arc is counted once at its source and once at its target, so Σ deg⁺(v) = Σ deg⁻(v) = number of arcs = 4.

Weighted graphs

A weighted graph assigns a number to each edge: formally a function w : E → ℝ. Weights might represent distance, travel time, or cost. A weighted graph may be directed or undirected. The meaning and allowed values of the weights depend on the model.

For an undirected example, let w(PQ) = 4, w(QR) = 2, and w(PR) = 9. The route P–Q–R has total weight 6; the single edge P–R has weight 9. Fewer edges need not mean lower total weight.

Distinguish: ordinary degree counts incident edge ends; it does not add edge weights. Unless stated otherwise, the representations and isomorphisms below concern finite, undirected, unweighted graphs.

Conventions matter. For a loopless multigraph, aij counts the edges between the two vertices. If loops are included, we use aii = twice the number of loops at vertex i; row sums then still give degrees. Other texts use a different diagonal convention, so state it explicitly.

Adapting representations

For a digraph, an adjacency list records outgoing neighbors; aij counts arcs from i to j. The matrix need not be symmetric. Row sums give out-degrees and column sums give in-degrees.

For a simple weighted graph, store (neighbor, weight) pairs in a list or edge weights in a matrix. Use a distinct symbol such as “—” for no edge if weight 0 is permitted. A weight matrix’s row sums generally do not equal ordinary degrees.

Discuss: Which representation makes neighbors easiest to read? Which lets you check a specific pair immediately? Which keeps each individual edge as a separate column?

Show discussion answer

Adjacency lists display neighbors directly; an adjacency matrix gives a direct entry for each pair; an incidence matrix records individual edges. Lists can be compact when relatively few pairs are joined, whereas an adjacency matrix always has |V|² entries.

3. The handshaking lemma, revisited

Read the incidence matrix in two directions. The row for v sums to deg(v), counting a loop twice. Each column sums to 2, because each edge has two ends.

Σv ∈ V deg(v) = Σv Σe Mv,e = Σe Σv Mv,e = 2|E|.

We have counted the same matrix entries by rows and by columns. For our graph the degrees, in vertex order, are (2, 4, 3, 2, 3), so their sum is 14 = 2 · 7. The descending degree sequence is (4, 3, 3, 2, 2).

Quick check: Which vertices have odd degree? Why must the number of odd-degree vertices be even?
Show answer

C and E have degree 3. In any finite undirected graph, the total degree is even. The even-degree terms contribute an even sum, so the sum of the odd-degree terms is even as well. That requires an even number of odd terms.

4. When are two graphs the same?

Moving vertices or renaming them may change a drawing without changing its connections. Isomorphism makes “same structure” precise.

Definition (simple undirected graphs). Graphs G = (VG, EG) and H = (VH, EH) are isomorphic, written G ≅ H, if there exists a bijection f : VG → VH such that for all distinct u,v ∈ VG,

{u,v} ∈ EG ⇔ {f(u),f(v)} ∈ EH.

A bijection is one-to-one and onto. The equivalence requires preservation of both adjacency and non-adjacency.

For undirected multigraphs with loops, require a vertex bijection preserving the number of edges between every pair u,v, including u = v for loops. For digraphs preserve the ordered arcs; for weighted isomorphisms require corresponding edges to have equal weights as well.

An explicit bijection

Let H have vertices {1,2,3,4,5} and edges {14,15,45,24,35,23,34}. Is it isomorphic to our five-vertex graph?

Every target occurs exactly once, so f is a bijection. The seven edges AB, AC, BC, BD, CE, DE, BE map respectively to 14, 15, 45, 24, 35, 23, 34: exactly the edge set of H. Since f is bijective and the image edge set equals EH, adjacency holds in both directions. Thus G ≅ H.

Invariants; necessary versus sufficient

An isomorphism invariant is a quantity or property unchanged by isomorphism. Examples include the numbers of vertices and edges, degree sequence, number of connected components, and number of cycles of each length, including triangles. A connected component is a maximal group of vertices joined to each other by paths.

Why are triangles preserved? If u,v,w form a triangle, their three distinct images form a triangle. Applying the inverse bijection shows that every triangle in the other graph arises this way.

G ≅ H ⇒ matching vertex counts, edge counts, degree sequences, and triangle counts.

Matching these quantities is necessary for isomorphism. Matching just a selection of invariants is generally not sufficient. A mismatch in even one invariant proves non-isomorphism; a match means we must continue investigating.

  1. Compare vertex counts, edge counts, and degree sequences.
  2. Look for a distinguishing invariant, such as a triangle in only one graph.
  3. If no mismatch is found, try a bijection, matching degrees and neighborhood structure.
  4. Verify the bijection preserves adjacency in both directions.

An explicit bijection satisfying the definition is sufficient to establish isomorphism. Failure of one attempted bijection does not prove that no isomorphism exists.

ML₃ ≅ K₃,₃: construct the isomorphism

The complete bipartite graph K₃,₃ has two parts X = {a₁,a₂,a₃} and Y = {b₁,b₂,b₃}. It has every edge aᵢbⱼ and no edges within either part. “Bipartite” means all edges run between the two parts.

In ML₃, split the vertices into {0,2,4} and {1,3,5}. Both cycle edges and opposite edges join an even index to an odd index. Each even vertex has three distinct neighbors, so it is adjacent to all three odd vertices.

Triangles settle the comparison

ML₃ has no triangles: each step switches between its two parts, so after three steps we cannot return to the starting vertex. CL₃ has the two triangles used in its construction. These are its only triangles: a triangle involving both layers would need a vertex with two neighbors in the other layer, but each vertex has only one such neighbor.

Therefore ML₃ ≇ CL₃. An isomorphism would carry a triangle of CL₃ to a triangle of ML₃, which is impossible. In particular, their equal vertex counts, edge counts, and degree sequences are not sufficient for isomorphism.

Reconnect to trails and cycles

Both graphs are cubic (3-regular): every vertex has degree 3. Neither graph is Eulerian: an Eulerian circuit would require all degrees even. Neither has an open Eulerian trail either, because each has six odd-degree vertices rather than exactly two.

Both are Hamiltonian: each has a cycle visiting every vertex exactly once before returning to its start. Here are explicit Hamiltonian cycles:

  • ML₃: 0–1–2–3–4–5–0.
  • CL₃: p₀–p₁–p₂–q₂–q₁–q₀–p₀.

Check each consecutive pair against the construction. An Eulerian circuit uses every edge exactly once; a Hamiltonian cycle visits every vertex exactly once. These are different requirements.

Exit ticket: In two sentences, explain what evidence is enough to prove two graphs are isomorphic and what evidence is enough to prove they are not. Use “bijection” and “invariant.”
Show a possible response

A bijection preserving adjacency in both directions proves isomorphism. A difference in any isomorphism invariant proves non-isomorphism.
