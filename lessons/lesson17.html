Lesson 17: Graph Representations and Isomorphism

1. Directed and weighted graphs

Last time we used undirected edges: AB and BA describe the same connection. Some situations require more information.

Directed graphs

A directed graph (digraph) has edges called arcs, with a specified source and target. An arc \((u,v)\), written \(u \to v\), need not be accompanied by \(v \to u\). For a simple loopless digraph, the arc set is a subset of \(\{(u,v) \in V \times V : u \ne v\}\).

Examples include one-way streets and links between web pages. A directed walk must follow each arrow. The out-degree \(\deg^+(v)\) counts arcs leaving \(v\); the in-degree \(\deg^-(v)\) counts arcs entering \(v\). A directed loop, if allowed, contributes one to both the out-degree and the in-degree.

Example. On vertices P, Q, R, take arcs P → Q, Q → R, R → P, P → R. List the in-degree and out-degree of each vertex. Is P → R → Q a directed walk?

Show answer

P: in 1, out 2. Q: in 1, out 1. R: in 2, out 1. The proposed walk fails because R → Q is absent. Each arc is counted once at its source and once at its target, so \(\sum_v \deg^+(v) = \sum_v \deg^-(v) = 4\).

Weighted graphs

A weighted graph assigns a number to each edge: formally a function \(w : E \to \mathbb{R}\). Weights might represent distance, travel time, or cost. A weighted graph may be directed or undirected. The meaning and allowed values of the weights depend on the model.

For an undirected example, let \(w(PQ) = 4\), \(w(QR) = 2\), and \(w(PR) = 9\). The route P–Q–R has total weight \(4 + 2 = 6\); the single edge P–R has weight 9. Fewer edges need not mean lower total weight.

Distinguish: ordinary degree counts incident edge ends; it does not add edge weights. Unless stated otherwise, the representations and isomorphisms below concern finite, undirected, unweighted graphs.

2. One graph, three representations

Use this simple graph throughout this section: no loops and no parallel edges.

\[ V = \{A, B, C, D, E\}, \qquad E = \{\mathrm{AB}, \mathrm{AC}, \mathrm{BC}, \mathrm{BD}, \mathrm{CE}, \mathrm{DE}, \mathrm{BE}\}. \]

Incidence matrix

A vertex is incident to an edge when it is an endpoint. Choose vertex order A, B, C, D, E and edge order \(e_1 = \mathrm{AB}\), \(e_2 = \mathrm{AC}\), \(e_3 = \mathrm{BC}\), \(e_4 = \mathrm{BD}\), \(e_5 = \mathrm{CE}\), \(e_6 = \mathrm{DE}\), \(e_7 = \mathrm{BE}\). The incidence matrix \(M\) has one row per vertex and one column per edge; \(M_{v,e}\) is 1 when \(v\) is an endpoint of \(e\) and 0 otherwise.

Conventions matter. For a loopless multigraph, \(a_{ij}\) counts the edges between the two vertices. If loops are included, we use \(a_{ii} = \) twice the number of loops at vertex \(i\); row sums then still give degrees. Other texts use a different diagonal convention, so state it explicitly.

Adapting representations

For a digraph, an adjacency list records outgoing neighbors; \(a_{ij}\) counts arcs from \(i\) to \(j\). The matrix need not be symmetric. Row sums give out-degrees and column sums give in-degrees.

For a simple weighted graph, store (neighbor, weight) pairs in a list or edge weights in a matrix. Use a distinct symbol such as “—” for no edge if weight 0 is permitted. A weight matrix’s row sums generally do not equal ordinary degrees.

Discuss: Which representation makes neighbors easiest to read? Which lets you check a specific pair immediately? Which keeps each individual edge as a separate column?

Show discussion answer

Adjacency lists display neighbors directly; an adjacency matrix gives a direct entry for each pair; an incidence matrix records individual edges. Lists can be compact when relatively few pairs are joined, whereas an adjacency matrix always has \(|V|^2\) entries.

3. The handshaking lemma, revisited

Read the incidence matrix in two directions. The row for \(v\) sums to \(\deg(v)\), counting a loop twice. Each column sums to 2, because each edge has two ends.

\[ \sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) = \sum_{v} \sum_{e} M_{v,e} = \sum_{e} \sum_{v} M_{v,e} = 2|E|. \]

We have counted the same matrix entries by rows and by columns. For our graph the degrees, in vertex order, are \( (2, 4, 3, 2, 3) \), so their sum is \( 14 = 2 \cdot 7 \). The descending degree sequence is \( (4, 3, 3, 2, 2) \).

Exercise. Which vertices have odd degree? Why must the number of odd-degree vertices be even?

Show answer

C and E have degree 3. In any finite undirected graph, the total degree is even. The even-degree terms contribute an even sum, so the sum of the odd-degree terms is even as well. That requires an even number of odd terms.

4. When are two graphs the same?

In what ways are the following graphs similar? In what ways are they different?

Consider the drawings, vertex labels, and connections separately. If we are allowed to rename vertices, do these pictures describe the same structure? Which vertex must correspond to the center in each picture?

Show discussion notes

Each graph has four vertices, three edges, and one vertex of degree 3 joined to three vertices of degree 1. Their positions and the label on the center differ: v₂ in Graph 1, v₃ in Graph 2, and v₁ in Graph 3. As graphs with labels required to stay fixed, their edge sets differ; allowing a relabeling makes all three structurally the same.

For example, from Graph 1 to Graph 2 use \(v_1 \mapsto v_1\), \(v_2 \mapsto v_3\), \(v_3 \mapsto v_2\), \(v_4 \mapsto v_4\). The three edges \(v_2v_1\), \(v_2v_3\), \(v_2v_4\) become exactly \(v_3v_1\), \(v_3v_2\), \(v_3v_4\). The centers correspond and the leaves correspond.

Moving vertices or renaming them may change a drawing without changing its connections. Isomorphism makes “same structure” precise.

Definition (simple undirected graphs). Graphs \(G = (V_G, E_G)\) and \(H = (V_H, E_H)\) are isomorphic, written \(G \cong H\), if there exists a bijection \(f : V_G \to V_H\) such that for all distinct \(u,v \in V_G\),
\[ \{u,v\} \in E_G \iff \{f(u),f(v)\} \in E_H. \]
A bijection is one-to-one and onto. The equivalence requires preservation of both adjacency and non-adjacency.

For undirected multigraphs with loops, require a vertex bijection preserving the number of edges between every pair \(u,v\), including the number of loops at each vertex. For digraphs preserve the ordered arcs; for weighted isomorphisms require corresponding edges to have equal weights as well.

An explicit bijection

Let H have vertices {1,2,3,4,5} and edges {14,15,45,24,35,23,34}. Is it isomorphic to our five-vertex graph?

Every target occurs exactly once, so \(f\) is a bijection. The seven edges AB, AC, BC, BD, CE, DE, BE map respectively to 14, 15, 45, 24, 35, 23, 34: exactly the edge set of H. Since \(f\) is bijective and the image edge set equals \(E_H\), adjacency holds in both directions. Thus \(G \cong H\).

Invariants; necessary versus sufficient

An isomorphism invariant is a quantity or property unchanged by isomorphism. Examples include the numbers of vertices and edges, degree sequence, number of connected components, and number of cycles of each length, including triangles. A connected component is a maximal group of vertices joined to each other by paths.

Isomorphic graphs have the same number of triangles, which may be zero. To see why, suppose \(f\) is an isomorphism from \(G\) to \(H\) and suppose \(G\) contains a triangle with vertices \(u, v, w\). The images \(f(u), f(v), f(w)\) are distinct because \(f\) is one-to-one, and the three edges joining them exist because \(f\) preserves adjacency. Thus they form a triangle in \(H\). The inverse isomorphism gives the reverse correspondence, so triangles in \(G\) and \(H\) are paired exactly. In particular, if \(G\) has no triangles, neither does \(H\).

\[ G \cong H \;\Rightarrow\; \text{equal vertex counts, edge counts, degree sequences, and triangle counts.} \]

Matching these quantities is necessary for isomorphism. Matching just a selection of invariants is generally not sufficient. A mismatch in even one invariant proves non-isomorphism; a match means we must continue investigating.

  1. Compare vertex counts, edge counts, and degree sequences.
  2. Look for a distinguishing invariant, such as a triangle in only one graph.
  3. If no mismatch is found, try a bijection, matching degrees and neighborhood structure.
  4. Verify the bijection preserves adjacency in both directions.

An explicit bijection satisfying the definition is sufficient to establish isomorphism. Failure of one attempted bijection does not prove that no isomorphism exists.

A short comparison exercise

Are these two graphs isomorphic? First compare their numbers of vertices and edges and their degree sequences. Then look at the neighbors of the unique vertex of degree 3. Give a reason that applies to every possible bijection.

Show hint

An isomorphism must take the only degree-3 vertex to the only degree-3 vertex. How many degree-1 neighbors does that vertex have in each graph?

Show solution

Both graphs have six vertices, five edges, and degree sequence (3,2,2,1,1,1). These matches do not settle the question.

Any isomorphism would have to send b to 3 because each is the unique vertex of degree 3. But b has two degree-1 neighbors, a and f, while 3 has only one, 6. A bijection preserving adjacency also preserves vertex degrees, so it cannot map both of those leaves to degree-1 neighbors of 3. Therefore G and H are not isomorphic.

This is a local structural difference: the degrees of the neighbors of corresponding vertices must match as well as the overall degree sequences.

5. A deceptive pair: ML₃ and CL₃

Precise constructions

The name Möbius ladder comes from the Möbius strip. Imagine bending a straight ladder with three rungs into a loop and connecting its ends. Joining the ends without a twist produces the circular ladder CL₃; giving the ladder a half-twist before joining the ends produces the Möbius ladder ML₃. We can describe both graphs precisely by listing their edges.

The Möbius ladder \(\ML_3\) starts with the cycle 0–1–2–3–4–5–0 and adds the three opposite chords 03, 14, 25.

The circular ladder \(\CL_3\), or triangular prism graph, consists of triangles p₀p₁p₂p₀ and q₀q₁q₂q₀, together with rungs p₀q₀, p₁q₁, p₂q₂.

ML₃ ≅ K₃,₃: construct the isomorphism

The complete bipartite graph \(K_{3,3}\) has two parts \(X = \{a_1,a_2,a_3\}\) and \(Y = \{b_1,b_2,b_3\}\). It has every edge \(a_i b_j\) and no edges within either part. “Bipartite” means all edges run between the two parts.

In \(\ML_3\), split the vertices into \(\{0,2,4\}\) and \(\{1,3,5\}\). Both cycle edges and opposite chords join an even index to an odd index. Each even vertex has three distinct neighbors, so it is adjacent to all three odd vertices.

To prove the isomorphism, we construct a bijection and check every pair of vertices.

Triangles settle the comparison

\(\ML_3\) has no triangles: each step switches between its two parts, so after three steps we cannot return to the starting vertex. \(\CL_3\) has the two triangles used in its construction. These are its only triangles: a triangle involving both layers would need a vertex with two neighbors in the other layer, but each vertex has only one such neighbor.

Therefore \(\ML_3 \not\cong \CL_3\). An isomorphism would carry a triangle of \(\CL_3\) to a triangle of \(\ML_3\), which is impossible. In particular, their equal vertex counts, edge counts, and degree sequences are not sufficient for isomorphism.

Reconnect to trails and cycles

Both graphs are cubic (3-regular): every vertex has degree 3. Neither graph is Eulerian: an Eulerian circuit would require all degrees even. Neither has an open Eulerian trail either, because each has six odd-degree vertices rather than exactly two.

Both are Hamiltonian: each has a cycle visiting every vertex exactly once before returning to its start. Here are explicit Hamiltonian cycles:

  • \(\ML_3\): 0–1–2–3–4–5–0.
  • \(\CL_3\): p₀–p₁–p₂–q₂–q₁–q₀–p₀.

Each consecutive pair is an edge in the corresponding construction. An Eulerian circuit uses every edge exactly once; a Hamiltonian cycle visits every vertex exactly once. These are different requirements.
