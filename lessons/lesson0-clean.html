Warm-Up: The Game of 15

We begin with a game for two players.

  • Players take turns choosing integers from 1 through 9.
  • Once a number has been chosen, it cannot be chosen again.
  • A player wins by collecting three numbers whose sum is 15.
  • A player may use any three of the numbers they have collected.
  • If neither player succeeds, the game ends in a tie.

Example

  • Player 1 chooses 8.
  • Player 2 chooses 5.
  • Player 1 chooses 2.
  • Player 2 chooses 6.
  • Player 1 chooses 4. The sum (8+2+4=14), so Player 1 has not yet won.
  • Player 2 chooses 9. None of Player 2's triples sums to 15.
  • Player 1 chooses 3 and wins because (8+4+3=15).

Pair Up

  1. Introduce yourselves: name, pronouns, year, major, interests, or anything else you would like to share.
  2. Play several games. Take turns going first.
  3. Look for a strategy. Is there a particularly strong first move? How can you block an opponent?
  4. Write your proposed strategy clearly enough that another pair could follow it.
  5. Compare strategies with another pair. Does this game remind you of another game?

The Hidden Structure

The game may look like an arithmetic puzzle, but arrange the numbers in this square:

\[ \begin{array}{|c|c|c|} \hline 8 & 1 & 6 \\ \hline 3 & 5 & 7 \\ \hline 4 & 9 & 2 \\ \hline \end{array} \]

Every row, column, and diagonal sums to 15. Therefore, collecting three numbers whose sum is 15 is equivalent to occupying a row, column, or diagonal in tic-tac-toe.

The two games look different, but they have the same underlying structure. Recognizing and describing hidden structures like this is an important part of discrete mathematics.

What Is Discrete Mathematics?

Discrete mathematics studies objects that come in separate pieces: integers, logical statements, sets, arrangements, graphs, and algorithms. Unlike quantities that vary continuously, these objects can often be listed, counted, or connected in distinct ways.

In this course, finding an answer is only part of the work. We will also ask why an answer must be correct, whether a pattern continues, and how to communicate an argument so that someone else can follow it.

Mathematical Writing

Mathematics is communicated through writing. A page of correct calculations is not yet a clear mathematical argument if the reader does not know what is being calculated or why.

What Is Missing?

Consider the following proposed solution:

\[ \begin{aligned} x^2+(x+2)^2 &=100 \\ 2x^2+4x+4 &=100 \\ x^2+2x-48 &=0 \\ (x+8)(x-6) &=0 \\ x &=-8 \text{ or } x=6 \\ x>0 &\implies x=6 \end{aligned} \]

The algebra is correct, but the writing is not sufficient. We do not know what problem is being solved, what (x) represents, or why (x) must be positive. The relationships between the lines are left for the reader to infer, and the proposed solution never answers a stated question.

A Clearer Solution

Problem. Find two positive numbers that differ by 2 and whose squares sum to 100.

Solution. Let (x) be the smaller number. The larger number is then (x+2). Because the sum of their squares is 100,

\[ x^2+(x+2)^2=100. \]

Simplifying and factoring gives

\[ \begin{aligned} 2x^2+4x+4 &=100,\\ x^2+2x-48 &=0,\\ (x+8)(x-6) &=0. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore, (x=-8) or (x=6). Since the numbers must be positive, we discard (x=-8). Thus, the smaller number is 6 and the larger number is 8. Indeed,

\[ 6^2+8^2=36+64=100. \]

Answer. The two numbers are 6 and 8.

Some Principles of Good Mathematical Writing

  • State the problem or claim.
  • Define variables and state assumptions.
  • Use complete sentences to connect calculations and explain reasoning.
  • Justify important steps.
  • Conclude by answering the original question.
  • Check the result when a useful verification is available.

For more advice, see Francis Su's Guidelines for Good Mathematical Writing.

A Brief Introduction to LaTeX

We will use LaTeX to write mathematics in this course. LaTeX is a markup language: instead of selecting mathematical symbols from a menu, you type commands that describe the notation and document structure.

In class, we will open a starter file, make a small change, compile it into a PDF, and submit the result through GitHub. You do not need to create a document from scratch.

Setup instructions, examples, and a command reference are available on the LaTeX Resources page.
