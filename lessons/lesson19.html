Lesson 19: Planarity and Euler’s Formula — Guided Self-Study

1. A self-study route

In Lesson 18, we separated a graph's adjacency structure from the appearance of a particular drawing. This lesson asks a new question: can we draw the graph with no edge crossings at all? Trees will provide the starting point for a formula relating vertices, edges, and regions. That formula will then prove that some graphs cannot be drawn without crossings, no matter how cleverly we try.

Allow about 75–90 minutes. Read each section in order, try each self-check on paper before opening its answer, and finish with the exercises. You will need the definitions of a simple graph, a cycle, a tree, a spanning tree, and a bipartite graph. All graphs here are finite, simple, and undirected unless a statement explicitly says otherwise.

By the end, you should be able to distinguish a planar graph from a plane drawing, count faces including the exterior, apply and explain Euler's formula, prove the nonplanarity of \(K_5\) and \(K_{3,3}\), and state what the Four Color Theorem guarantees.

2. A crossing in the picture—or a crossing we cannot avoid?

A graph is planar if it has a drawing in the plane in which vertices are distinct points and edges are simple curves that meet only at common endpoints. No edge may pass through a vertex other than one of its endpoints. Edges may bend; they need not be straight.

We will call a particular crossing-free drawing a plane drawing. A graph is planar if at least one such drawing exists.

The left-hand drawing has a crossing. The right-hand drawing proves that \(K_4\) is planar. In both drawings the edge set is \(\{ab,ac,ad,bc,bd,cd\}\). The unmarked crossing on the left is not a fifth vertex.

To prove a graph planar, one valid crossing-free drawing is enough. To prove it nonplanar, one unsuccessful drawing is not enough: we must rule out every possible drawing.

Faces: remember the outside

When a planar graph is drawn without edges crossing, its vertices and edges divide the plane into regions. Each region is called a face. We include the unbounded “outside” region, called the exterior face. The crossing-free drawing of \(K_4\) above has three bounded faces and one exterior face, for a total of four.

Although the shapes of the faces depend on the drawing, their total number is the same in every crossing-free drawing of a given planar graph. We may therefore speak of the number of faces of a planar graph. Euler's formula below will explain why this number does not change.

A tree can be drawn without crossings and has just one face. Its branches do not enclose any region. For example, start with one vertex and build a tree by adding a leaf at a time, placing each new edge locally without creating a crossing or enclosing a region.

Self-check 1. A square has both diagonals drawn, crossing at an unmarked point. May we count its four triangular-looking regions as faces of a plane drawing of \(K_4\)?

Check your reasoning

No. This is not a plane drawing because two edges cross. If we deliberately replace the crossing by a new vertex, we get a different graph with five vertices and eight edges, and its plane drawing has five faces (four bounded and the exterior). Those are not the vertex, edge, or face counts of a plane drawing of \(K_4\).

3. Discovering Euler's formula

Explore before reading the formula. Draw a pentagon with vertices \(a,b,c,d,e\), using only its five sides. Count its vertices, edges, and faces, including the outside. Then make these two changes, keeping the drawing crossing-free:

  1. Add the diagonal \(ac\) inside the pentagon.
  2. Add a new vertex \(x\) outside the pentagon and join it only to \(b\).

Record the counts after each step. Which of \(n\), \(m\), and \(f\) change together? Can you find an expression involving all three that stays constant?

The three rows are \((5,5,2)\), \((5,6,3)\), and \((6,7,3)\). Adding the diagonal splits one face into two: edges and faces each increase by 1. Adding the new leaf increases vertices and edges by 1 but leaves the faces unchanged. In all three rows, \(n-m+f=2\).

Theorem. For a finite nonempty connected plane graph with n vertices, m edges, and f faces,

\[\boxed{n-m+f=2.}\]

Equivalently, \(f=m-n+2\). The formula concerns a crossing-free drawing, and f includes its exterior face.

Why the formula holds: reduce to a tree

Recall the constructive spanning-tree argument from Lecture 18: remove an edge from a cycle, keeping all vertices. The graph stays connected because the rest of that cycle still joins the endpoints of the deleted edge.

In a plane drawing, a cycle is a simple closed curve separating an inside from an outside. A cycle edge borders two distinct faces, one on each side. Removing that edge merges precisely those two faces into one. Thus n stays the same, while m and f each decrease by 1. The quantity \(n-m+f\) is unchanged.

Continue removing cycle edges until a spanning tree remains. If the original graph has n vertices, this tree has \(n-1\) edges and one face. For the tree,

\[n-(n-1)+1=2.\]

Since each removal preserved the expression, the original plane graph also satisfies \(n-m+f=2\). ∎

This proof explains the pattern in the experiment: deleting a cycle edge reverses the step of splitting a face, and the final tree supplies the starting count.

Worked example: the cube graph

This drawing has \(n=8\), \(m=12\), and \(f=6\): the inner square, four regions between the squares, and the exterior. Hence \(8-12+6=2\). The usual three-dimensional picture of a cube represents the same graph, but the drawing above makes its planarity and its faces explicit.

Self-check 2. A connected plane graph has 10 vertices and 15 edges. How many faces are bounded? A tree has 10 vertices; how many faces does its plane drawing have?

Check your answer

The first graph has \(f=15-10+2=7\) faces, of which 6 are bounded. The tree has 9 edges and \(f=9-10+2=1\) face, its exterior. It has no bounded faces.

4. Each edge has two sides

We now have a way to predict how many faces a connected graph would have if it were planar. To test that prediction, count how many edge sides surround those faces.

Each edge has two sides. When we count the sides around every face, including the outside, every edge is counted twice. Therefore the total number of edge sides around all faces is \(2m\).

In the plane drawing of \(K_4\), each of the four faces is surrounded by three edges. Counting around the faces gives \(4\cdot3=12\); counting two sides for each of the six edges also gives \(2\cdot6=12\).

If every face requires at least three edge sides, then \(3f\le2m\). If every face requires at least four, then \(4f\le2m\). We will apply these observations directly to two graphs.

What happens when an edge has the same face on both sides?

Both sides still count. This happens for a bridge, such as the edge \(cd\) below.

The inside triangular face uses three edge sides. Tracing the outside along \(a,b,c,d,c,a\) uses five, because \(cd\) appears twice. The total is \(3+5=8=2m\). Thus a face boundary need not be a cycle; count edge occurrences, including repetitions.

5. Two graphs that cannot be drawn without crossings

The complete graph K₅

Claim. \(K_5\) is nonplanar.

Proof by contradiction. Suppose \(K_5\) had a crossing-free drawing. It has \(n=5\) vertices and \(m=10\) edges, so Euler's formula would give

\[5-10+f=2,\qquad f=7.\]

Each face would need at least three edge sides: there are no loops or parallel edges, and every edge belongs to a cycle, so no edge is a bridge. Seven faces would therefore require at least \(3\cdot7=21\) edge sides. But ten edges supply only \(2\cdot10=20\). This would force

\[21\le20,\]

which is impossible. Thus \(K_5\) is nonplanar. ∎

The complete bipartite graph K₃,₃

The two parts are \(\{a,b,c\}\) and \(\{x,y,z\}\). Each vertex in the first part is joined to all three vertices in the second, giving six vertices and nine edges. Think of three houses and three utilities: can each house be joined to every utility without crossings?

Claim. \(K_{3,3}\) is nonplanar.

Proof by contradiction. Suppose it had a crossing-free drawing. Euler's formula would give

\[6-9+f=2,\qquad f=5.\]

Every edge belongs to a cycle, so there are no bridges. The graph is simple, and it has no triangles: a walk alternates between the two parts, so a cycle must have even length. Each face would therefore require at least four edge sides. Five faces would need at least \(4\cdot5=20\) sides, but nine edges supply only \(2\cdot9=18\). We would obtain

\[20\le18,\]

again impossible. Thus \(K_{3,3}\) is nonplanar. ∎

The common strategy

  1. Assume that a crossing-free drawing exists.
  2. Use Euler's formula to find its required number of faces.
  3. Find the minimum number of edge sides needed around those faces.
  4. Compare that minimum with the available \(2m\) sides.

The difference between the two proofs is the minimum face size: three for \(K_5\), four for \(K_{3,3}\). The contradiction rules out every possible drawing, not just the drawings shown above.

Self-check 3. Why would using only \(3f\le2m\) fail to prove that \(K_{3,3}\) is nonplanar? What additional feature makes the proof work?

Check your reasoning

With \(f=5\) and \(m=9\), the weaker count says \(15\le18\), which is true and gives no contradiction. The absence of triangles forces at least four sides per face, giving the impossible inequality \(20\le18\).

A useful extension. A graph containing \(K_5\) or \(K_{3,3}\) as a subgraph is also nonplanar. Otherwise, erasing the extra vertices and edges from a crossing-free drawing would give a crossing-free drawing of the nonplanar subgraph.

6. From planar graphs to four colors

A proper vertex coloring assigns a color to each vertex so that adjacent vertices receive different colors. Nonadjacent vertices may share a color. A color can be represented by a number or a name; the condition is about equality and difference, not about the appearance of an ink color.

The cycle \(C_4\) needs only two colors. A triangle needs three, because its vertices are pairwise adjacent. The planar graph \(K_4\) needs four, for the same reason: all four vertices are pairwise adjacent.

Four Color Theorem. Every finite simple planar graph has a proper vertex coloring using at most four colors.

“At most four” does not mean that every planar graph needs four colors. The examples above need two, three, and four, respectively. The theorem asserts the existence of a coloring; it does not say that an arbitrary sequence of color choices will succeed. We will study coloring methods in Lecture 20. The proof of the Four Color Theorem is much deeper than Euler's formula and is not included here.

Why this is also a map-coloring theorem

For an ordinary map whose countries are connected regions, regard each region as a vertex, and join two vertices when their regions share a boundary segment of positive length. Touching at just one point does not make two regions adjacent. The resulting adjacency graph is planar; proper vertex coloring assigns different colors to neighboring regions. Repeated edges may be suppressed because they impose the same coloring constraint.

The connected-region assumption matters: if disconnected territories must share one color because they belong to the same country, the usual four-color statement need not apply. We use the standard connected-region model here.

Self-check 4. Does a proper four-coloring prove that a graph is planar? Does a planar drawing guarantee a proper three-coloring?

Check your answer

No to both. The nonplanar graph \(K_{3,3}\) is bipartite and has a proper two-coloring, so it certainly can be colored using at most four colors. Conversely, \(K_4\) is planar but requires four colors. The Four Color Theorem is an implication from planarity to colorability, not an equivalence.

Optional: two useful edge bounds

The counting arguments above can be packaged into formulas. These are convenient shortcuts once you understand the direct face counts.

Derive the general bound m ≤ 3n − 6

For a connected simple planar graph with \(n\ge3\), every face boundary has at least three edge occurrences. A boundary of length 1 would require a loop; one of length 2 would require parallel edges or a connected graph consisting of just one edge. Our hypotheses exclude these cases. Therefore \(3f\le2m\). Substituting \(f=m-n+2\) gives

\[3(m-n+2)\le2m,\qquad\boxed{m\le3n-6}.\]

For example, \(K_5\) has 10 edges, exceeding \(3(5)-6=9\).

Derive the triangle-free bound m ≤ 2n − 4

If a connected simple planar graph has \(n\ge3\) and no triangles, every face boundary has at least four edge occurrences. Hence \(4f\le2m\), and

\[4(m-n+2)\le2m,\qquad\boxed{m\le2n-4}.\]

This applies in particular to bipartite graphs. For \(K_{3,3}\), it would require \(9\le2(6)-4=8\), a contradiction.

These bounds are necessary conditions, not tests that establish planarity. For example, attach a leaf to \(K_{3,3}\). The resulting graph satisfies both bounds, with 7 vertices and 10 edges, but remains nonplanar because it contains \(K_{3,3}\).
