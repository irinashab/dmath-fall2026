Exam 2: Study Guide and Practice

Discrete Mathematics · Fall 2026

Preparing for Exam 2

Go over the examples and exercises from the lectures. Rework examples without looking at the solutions, try the lecture exercises, and check that you can explain why each method works. Use the practice problems below to identify topics that need more review.

This is a study sheet, not a sample exam. The questions use a variety of formats and contexts; they do not predict the exact questions or organization of the exam.

The exam is 100 minutes. No calculators are allowed. An instructor-provided reference sheet will be supplied. Exact answers involving powers, factorials, and binomial coefficients may be left unevaluated.

Topics on the exam

  • Divisibility and congruence: use definitions, reason about implications, and give proofs or counterexamples.
  • The Euclidean algorithm: successive divisions, greatest common divisors, geometric interpretation using segments, and why the last nonzero remainder is the greatest common measure.
  • Mathematical induction: write a complete proof, including the algebra needed in the induction step.
  • Counting: addition and multiplication rules, repetition restrictions, complements, and inclusion–exclusion for two or three sets. Review factorials, permutations, and combinations as counting tools.
  • Second-order linear homogeneous recurrences with constant coefficients: compute terms, form the characteristic equation, find distinct roots, and use initial conditions to determine a closed formula.
  • Functions and relations: injectivity and composition of functions; reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity of relations; proofs and counterexamples.

Graphs are not on this exam.

The reference sheet includes basic definitions and formulas, including injectivity. You should know the definitions of reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity and how to organize an induction proof.

Practice problems

Show your reasoning. A numerical answer or a true/false label alone is not sufficient. Work on paper before opening the solutions.

Divisibility, congruence, and functions

  1. Let \(a,b,c\) be integers. Prove that if \(a\mid b\) and \(a\mid c\), then \(a\mid(3b-2c)\). Start from the definition of divisibility.
  2. A student claims that \(2x\equiv2y\pmod6\) always implies \(x\equiv y\pmod6\). Find a counterexample. Then prove that the hypothesis does imply \(x\equiv y\pmod3\).
  3. Let \(f,g:\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z\) be given by \(f(n)=3n-2\) and \(g(n)=n^2\).
    1. Prove that \(f\) is injective and show that \(g\) is not.
    2. Write a formula for \((f\circ g)(n)\). Is this composition injective? Justify your answer.

Common measure and induction

  1. A carpenter has strips of lengths 78 cm and 48 cm. Both strips must be cut into pieces of one common length, with no waste. Use successive Euclidean divisions to find the largest possible piece length, and determine the number of pieces from each strip. Explain what the first remainder represents as a segment.
  2. Suppose a segment of length \(L\) consists of \(q\) copies of a shorter segment of length \(S\), followed by a leftover segment of length \(r>0\). Explain why a positive length measures both \(L\) and \(S\) exactly if and only if it measures both \(S\) and \(r\) exactly. Use this observation to explain why the Euclidean algorithm preserves common measures and identifies the greatest one.
  3. Prove by induction that \(3\mid(4^n-1)\) for every integer \(n\ge0\).
  4. Prove by induction that \[\sum_{k=1}^{n}(2k-1)=n^2\qquad(n\ge1).\] Explain exactly where you use the induction hypothesis.

Counting

  1. An equipment label has four positions. Exactly two positions contain digits chosen from 0 through 9, and the other two contain letters chosen from A through Z. No digit or letter may be repeated. How many labels are possible? Explain each factor in your expression.
  2. A committee of three is chosen from five first-year students and four second-year students. Count committees containing at least one second-year student in two ways: by subtraction, and by separating the possible numbers of second-year students.
  3. Among 50 students, 24 study French, 20 study Spanish, and 18 study German. Of these, 10 study both French and Spanish, 8 both French and German, and 6 both Spanish and German; 4 study all three. The pairwise counts include students studying all three. How many study at least one of these languages? How many study none? Explain your inclusion–exclusion expression.

Recurrences and relations

  1. Consider the recurrence \(b_n=2b_{n-1}+3b_{n-2}\) for \(n\ge2\).
    1. Find the characteristic roots. Check by substitution that each corresponding geometric sequence satisfies the recurrence.
    2. With \(b_0=0\) and \(b_1=4\), find the constants in the general solution. Check your formula by computing \(b_2\) directly from the recurrence.
    3. Explain why the recurrence alone does not determine a unique sequence.
  2. Define a relation \(R\) on \(\mathbb Z\) by \(xRy\) if \(x-y\) is even. Determine whether it is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. Prove each conclusion using arbitrary integers.
  3. Construct a relation on \(\{a,b,c\}\) that is symmetric but not transitive. List its ordered pairs, explain why it is symmetric, and identify the specific pairs that show failure of transitivity.

Solutions for checking your work

Try each problem before opening its solution.

1. Divisibility

Write \(b=au\) and \(c=av\) for integers \(u,v\). Then \(3b-2c=a(3u-2v)\). Since \(3u-2v\) is an integer, \(a\mid(3b-2c)\).

2. Cancellation in a congruence

Take \(x=3,y=0\). Then \(2x-2y=6\) is divisible by 6, but \(x-y=3\) is not. In general, the hypothesis gives \(2(x-y)=6k\) for some integer \(k\), so \(x-y=3k\). Thus \(x\equiv y\pmod3\).

3. Injectivity and composition

If \(f(x)=f(y)\), then \(3x-2=3y-2\), so \(x=y\). Thus \(f\) is injective. But \(g(1)=g(-1)=1\), so \(g\) is not injective. The composition is \((f\circ g)(n)=3n^2-2\); it is not injective because its values at 1 and −1 are equal.

4. Cutting strips

The divisions are \[78=1\cdot48+30,\quad48=1\cdot30+18,\quad30=1\cdot18+12,\quad18=1\cdot12+6,\quad12=2\cdot6+0.\] The largest piece length is 6 cm, yielding 13 pieces and 8 pieces. The first remainder is the 30 cm left after laying one 48 cm segment along the 78 cm strip.

5. Preserving common measures

If \(L=ud\) and \(S=vd\) for positive integers \(u,v\), then \(r=L-qS=(u-qv)d\); since \(r>0\), its coefficient is a positive integer. Conversely, if \(S=vd\) and \(r=wd\), then \(L=(qv+w)d\). Thus the common measures are unchanged. At termination the shorter segment measures the longer exactly. It is the greatest common measure there, since any common measure must fit a positive whole number of times into that shorter segment. The preserved common measures give the same conclusion for the original segments.

6. Induction: divisibility

For \(n=0\), \(4^0-1=0\), which is divisible by 3. Suppose \(4^n-1=3t\) for an integer \(t\). Then \[4^{n+1}-1=4(4^n-1)+3=3(4t+1).\] Hence the claim holds for \(n+1\). By induction it holds for all integers \(n\ge0\).

7. Induction: sum of odd numbers

For \(n=1\), both sides equal 1. Suppose \(\sum_{k=1}^n(2k-1)=n^2\). Then \[\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}(2k-1)=n^2+(2(n+1)-1)=n^2+2n+1=(n+1)^2.\] The induction hypothesis replaces the sum of the first \(n\) terms by \(n^2\). The identity follows for every \(n\ge1\) by induction.

8. Equipment labels

Choose the two digit positions, fill them with two distinct digits in order, and fill the remaining positions with two distinct letters in order: \[\binom42\,(10\cdot9)(26\cdot25).\]

9. Committees

Subtract the all-first-year committees from all committees, or separate committees with one, two, or three second-year students: \[\binom93-\binom53=\binom41\binom52+\binom42\binom51+\binom43\binom50=74.\]

10. Languages

Inclusion–exclusion gives \(24+20+18-10-8-6+4=42\) students studying at least one language. Subtract the pairwise intersections to correct double counting, then add the triple intersection back. Thus \(50-42=8\) study none.

11. Recurrence

The characteristic equation is \(r^2-2r-3=(r-3)(r+1)=0\), with roots 3 and −1. For either root \(r\), \(2r^{n-1}+3r^{n-2}=r^{n-2}(2r+3)=r^n\), so \(r^n\) satisfies the recurrence.

The general solution is \(b_n=A3^n+B(-1)^n\). The initial conditions give \(A+B=0\) and \(3A-B=4\), so \(A=1,B=-1\). Thus \(b_n=3^n-(-1)^n\). Directly, \(b_2=2(4)+3(0)=8\), agreeing with \(9-1=8\). Different initial values give different constants and different sequences.

12. A relation on the integers

Reflexive: \(x-x=0\) is even for every integer \(x\). Symmetric: if \(x-y=2k\), then \(y-x=2(-k)\) is even. Transitive: if \(x-y=2k\) and \(y-z=2\ell\), then \(x-z=2(k+\ell)\) is even. Hence all three properties hold.

13. Constructing a relation

One example is \(R=\{(a,b),(b,a)\}\). Each pair has its reverse, so \(R\) is symmetric. But \((a,b)\in R\) and \((b,a)\in R\), while \((a,a)\notin R\), so it is not transitive.
