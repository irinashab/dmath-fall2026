1. Recap: Modular Arithmetic

Last time we learned:

  • Division algorithm: \(n = qm + r\) with \(0 \le r < m\).
  • Congruence: \(x \equiv y \pmod n\) iff \(n \mid (x-y)\).
  • Congruence classes: the \(n\) disjoint sets of numbers with the same remainder mod \(n\).
  • Addition: if \(a \equiv x\) and \(b \equiv y \pmod n\), then \(a+b \equiv x+y \pmod n\).

Today we apply this to two applications of quotient and remainder: weekdays (modulo 7) and number representations (digits and carries). Assume every modulus is positive.

2. Why Our Calendar Is Weird

To understand leap years — and why the Doomsday algorithm handles them the way it does — we need a little history.

The Roman calendar (before 46 BC)

A lunar system of about 355 days. Extra months were occasionally added by priests, often for political reasons. The result was chaos: the calendar drifted out of sync with the seasons.

The Julian reform (46 BC)

Julius Caesar, advised by the astronomer Sosigenes, switched to a solar calendar:

  • 365 days per year.
  • An extra day every 4 years — a leap day.
  • Average year length: \(365.25\) days.

But the true solar year is about \(365.2422\) days. The difference — about 11 minutes per year — accumulated over centuries.

The Gregorian reform (1582)

By the 16th century, the Julian calendar had drifted about 10 days. Pope Gregory XIII introduced:

  1. A one-time reset: in the countries that adopted the reform immediately, October 4, 1582 was followed by October 15. Other countries adopted it later.
  2. A refined leap year rule:
    • A year divisible by 4 is a leap year, unless it is a century year.
    • Century years divisible by 400 are leap years.
    • So 1900 was not a leap year, but 2000 was.

Counting leap days: the average calendar year over a 400-year cycle is exactly

\[ 365 + \tfrac{1}{4} - \tfrac{1}{100} + \tfrac{1}{400} = 365.2425 \ \text{days}, \]

which is very close to the approximate solar year of \(365.2422\) days. The Gregorian rules are just a clever way to keep the calendar synced with the sun.

This is the calendar used below, even when extended backward to dates before local adoption. Historical local dates may use a different calendar. See the U.S. Naval Observatory’s leap-year explanation.

Quick check: Which of 1900, 2000, 2024, and 2100 are leap years?

Answer

2000 and 2024. The century years 1900 and 2100 are not divisible by 400.

3. The Doomsday Algorithm

The Doomsday algorithm (developed by John Conway) finds the day of the week for any date in the Gregorian calendar.

Core idea

Every year has a Doomsday: a specific weekday on which several easy-to-remember dates always fall. Once you know the year's Doomsday, you can find any date by counting from the nearest key date.

Key dates

CategoryMnemonicDates
Jan / Feb Jan 3 / 4; last day of February Jan 3 in common years / Jan 4 in leap years; Feb 28 in common years / Feb 29 in leap years
MarchMarch 14Mar 14 in every year
Even months "4/4, 6/6, 8/8, 10/10, 12/12" Apr 4, Jun 6, Aug 8, Oct 10, Dec 12
Odd months "I work 9-to-5 at the 7-11" May 9, Sep 5, Jul 11, Nov 7
Holidays Independence Day, Halloween, Boxing Day Jul 4, Oct 31, Dec 26

Why do the key dates share a weekday?

They are separated by whole numbers of weeks. From April 4 to June 6, the elapsed time is

\[ (30-4)+31+6=63=9\cdot7\text{ days}. \]

So June 6 has the same weekday as April 4. The other key dates work for the same reason: their offsets from one another are multiples of 7.

The January adjustment also has a reason. In a common year, January 3 to February 28 is \((31-3)+28=56\) days. In a leap year, January 4 to February 29 is \((31-4)+29=56\) days. Both are exactly eight weeks.

Numbering the days of the week

Throughout this lecture, we use the following numbering:

Weekday numbers modulo 7
SundayMondayTuesdayWednesdayThursdayFridaySaturday
0123456

Add 1 to move forward one day, then reduce modulo 7. For example, the day after Saturday is \((6+1)\bmod7=0\), Sunday. Other numbering conventions are possible, but the anchor numbers and calculations below all use Sunday = 0.

Century anchors

YearDoomsday
1600Tuesday (2)
1700Sunday (0)
1800Friday (5)
1900Wednesday (3)
2000Tuesday (2)
2100Sunday (0)

Formula for a year within a century

Let \(Y\) be the last two digits of the year, and let \(L = \lfloor Y/4 \rfloor\). Then

\[ D = \big( D_{\text{century}} + Y + L \big) \bmod 7. \]

Once you know \(D\), any key date in that year falls on day \(D\). Add the signed number of days from a key date to the target, then reduce modulo 7. A target before the key date gives a negative offset.

Why does the year formula work?

Track the last day of February. From one year to the next, its weekday advances by 1, or by 2 if the new year is leap. Within the century, \(Y\) counts the ordinary shifts and \(\lfloor Y/4\rfloor\) counts the extra leap-day shifts. The century anchor already accounts for the century’s own leap-year status.

Why do the century anchors repeat? In 400 years there are 97 leap days, giving \(400\cdot365+97=146097=7\cdot20871\) days. Thus the Gregorian calendar repeats every 400 years.

4. Worked Examples

Example 1: May 15, 2020

  • Century anchor for 2000: \(D_{2000} = 2\) (Tuesday).
  • \(Y = 20\), \(L = \lfloor 20/4 \rfloor = 5\).
  • \(D_{2020} = (2 + 20 + 5) \bmod 7 = 27 \bmod 7 = 6\) → Saturday.
  • The May key date is May 9, which is a Saturday.
  • May 15 is 6 days after May 9: \(6 + 6 = 12 \equiv 5 \pmod 7\) → Friday.

Example 2: September 11, 1996

  • Century anchor for 1900: \(D_{1900} = 3\) (Wednesday).
  • \(Y = 96\), \(L = \lfloor 96/4 \rfloor = 24\).
  • \(D_{1996} = (3 + 96 + 24) \bmod 7 = 123 \bmod 7 = 4\) → Thursday.
  • The September key date is Sep 5, a Thursday.
  • Sep 11 is 6 days after Sep 5: \(4 + 6 = 10 \equiv 3 \pmod 7\) → Wednesday.

Example 3: November 22, 1963

  • Century anchor for 1900: \(D_{1900} = 3\) (Wednesday).
  • \(Y = 63\), \(L = \lfloor 63/4 \rfloor = 15\).
  • \(D_{1963} = (3 + 63 + 15) \bmod 7 = 81 \bmod 7 = 4\) → Thursday.
  • The November key date is Nov 7, a Thursday.
  • Nov 22 is 15 days after Nov 7: \(15 \bmod 7 = 1\), so Thursday + 1 = Friday.

Example 4: July 4, 1776

  • Century anchor for 1700: \(D_{1700} = 0\) (Sunday).
  • \(Y = 76\), \(L = \lfloor 76/4 \rfloor = 19\).
  • \(D_{1776} = (0 + 76 + 19) \bmod 7 = 95 \bmod 7 = 4\) → Thursday.
  • July 4 is itself a key date, so it was a Thursday.

5. Computers Count in Base 2

A bit has two possible values, 0 and 1. This does not mean that an entire computer repeats every two steps. Binary is positional notation: each digit records a remainder modulo 2, while higher places preserve the quotient.

Positional notation

Our usual decimal system writes a number like 3125 as

\[ 3125 = 3 \times 10^3 + 1 \times 10^2 + 2 \times 10^1 + 5 \times 10^0. \]

There is nothing special about 10. For an integer \(b\ge2\), in base \(b\), we use powers of \(b\) as place values and need \(b\) distinct digits.

  • Binary (base 2): digits 0 and 1. Place values are \(1, 2, 4, 8, 16, \ldots\)
  • Octal (base 8): digits 0–7. Place values \(1, 8, 64, 512, \ldots\)
  • Hexadecimal (base 16): digits 0–9 and A–F (A=10, B=11, …, F=15). Place values \(1, 16, 256, \ldots\)

Binary example

\[ 101101_2 = 1 \cdot 2^5 + 0 \cdot 2^4 + 1 \cdot 2^3 + 1 \cdot 2^2 + 0 \cdot 2^1 + 1 \cdot 2^0 = 32 + 8 + 4 + 1 = 45_{10}. \]

We write the subscript to indicate the base. When there's no subscript, assume base 10.

6. Converting Between Bases

Binary → decimal

Just expand the place values and add up the ones that have a 1.

Example: \(1010_2 = 8 + 0 + 2 + 0 = 10_{10}\).

Decimal → binary (repeated division)

For a positive integer, divide by 2 repeatedly until the quotient is 0, recording the remainders. Read them backward. Represent zero by the single digit 0.

Example: Convert 12 to binary.

  • \(12 \div 2 = 6\) remainder 0
  • \(6 \div 2 = 3\) remainder 0
  • \(3 \div 2 = 1\) remainder 1
  • \(1 \div 2 = 0\) remainder 1

Read remainders bottom to top: \(1100_2\). ✓

Why do we read the remainders backward?

Writing \(N=2q+r\), the remainder \(r\) is the units digit. Dividing \(q\) next finds the twos digit, then the fours digit, and so on. For 12, substitution gives \(12=2(2(2\cdot1+1)+0)+0=2^3+2^2\). The same procedure works in any base \(b\), dividing by \(b\) instead of 2.

Binary addition: remainder and carry

At each column, add its two digits and the incoming carry. The written digit is the sum modulo 2; the outgoing carry is the sum div 2. For example, \(1+1+1=3=2\cdot1+1\): write 1, carry 1.

   1011
+  0110
-------
  10001

Check in decimal: \(11+6=17\). Keeping the carry gives ordinary integer addition, rather than just arithmetic modulo 2.

Decimal → binary (largest power method)

Alternatively, subtract the largest power of 2 that fits, repeatedly.

Example: \(105_{10}\).

  • Largest power of 2 ≤ 105: 64. \(105 - 64 = 41\).
  • Largest power ≤ 41: 32. \(41 - 32 = 9\).
  • Largest power ≤ 9: 8. \(9 - 8 = 1\).
  • Largest power ≤ 1: 1. \(1 - 1 = 0\).

So we need 1s in the \(2^6, 2^5, 2^3, 2^0\) places: \(105_{10} = 1101001_2\).

7. Hex and Octal

Binary is verbose. Hex and octal are compact because each hex digit encodes 4 bits and each octal digit encodes 3 bits.

Hex ↔ binary

Every hex digit corresponds to exactly 4 bits:

0 → 00008 → 1000
1 → 00019 → 1001
2 → 0010A → 1010
3 → 0011B → 1011
4 → 0100C → 1100
5 → 0101D → 1101
6 → 0110E → 1110
7 → 0111F → 1111

Example: \(11101011_2\).

  • Group from the right in 4s: \(1110\;1011\).
  • \(1110_2 = E_{16}\), \(1011_2 = B_{16}\).
  • So \(11101011_2 = EB_{16}\).
  • Check: \(EB_{16} = 14 \cdot 16 + 11 = 235_{10}\). And \(11101011_2 = 128+64+32+8+2+1 = 235\). ✓

Example: \(AF_{16} = 1010\;1111_2\).

Optional: Octal ↔ binary

Every octal digit corresponds to exactly 3 bits. Group from the right in 3s (pad with leading zeros if needed).

Example: \(11101011_2\).

  • Pad to a multiple of 3: \(011\;101\;011\).
  • \(011_2 = 3_8\), \(101_2 = 5_8\), \(011_2 = 3_8\).
  • So \(11101011_2 = 353_8\).
  • Check: \(353_8 = 3 \cdot 64 + 5 \cdot 8 + 3 = 192 + 40 + 3 = 235\). ✓

8. Bits, Bytes, and Data

A bit is a single binary digit (0 or 1). A byte is 8 bits.

With 8 bits, there are \(2^8 = 256\) possible values. Common uses:

  • An unsigned byte represents 0 to 255.
  • A signed byte (using two's complement) represents −128 to 127.
  • With 8 bits per color channel, RGB uses 3 bytes per pixel and offers \(256^3=2^{24}\) color combinations (before compression, without transparency).

Data sizes

  • 1 byte = 8 bits
  • 1 kilobyte (kB) = 1000 bytes; 1 kibibyte (KiB) = 1024 bytes
  • 1 megabyte (MB) = \(10^6\) bytes
  • 1 gigabyte (GB) = \(10^9\) bytes
  • 1 terabyte (TB) = \(10^{12}\) bytes

The binary prefixes are kibi (KiB, \(2^{10}\)), mebi (MiB, \(2^{20}\)), gibi (GiB, \(2^{30}\)), tebi (TiB, \(2^{40}\)).

How many bits do we need?

If \(n\) bits give \(2^n\) distinct values, then to write a positive integer \(N\) in unsigned binary without leading zeros, we need:

\[ \text{bits}(N) = \lfloor \log_2 N \rfloor + 1. \]

For example, \(\log_2 500 \approx 8.97\), so 500 needs 9 bits.

Zero is conventionally written with one bit, 0; the logarithm formula does not apply to it. To store every integer from 0 through \(M\), choose the smallest positive integer \(n\) with \(2^n\ge M+1\). To label \(K\ge2\) distinct outcomes, you need \(\lceil\log_2 K\rceil\) bits.

Fixed-width wraparound

An unsigned \(n\)-bit representation has range \(0\) through \(2^n-1\). If an operation keeps only the lowest \(n\) bits, its result is reduced modulo \(2^n\). With 8 bits, \(255+1=256\) becomes \(00000000_2\), because \(256\bmod256=0\).

Java's int

A Java int is 32 bits, giving \(2^{32} \approx 4.3\) billion values. Signed, the range is \(-2^{31}\) to \(2^{31} - 1\), i.e. about \(\pm 2.1\) billion.

The Year 2038 problem. Systems that store Unix timestamps in a signed 32-bit integer count seconds from the start of 1970 UTC with a limited range. Its maximum is \(2^{31} - 1 = 2{,}147{,}483{,}647\), which is reached on January 19, 2038 at 03:14:07 UTC. After that, the counter overflows.

Switching to a 64-bit integer extends the range to about \(9 \times 10^{18}\) seconds — roughly 290 billion years.

Moral: exponential growth is dramatic. Adding one bit doubles the number of representable values.

9. Synthesis: Remainders, Digits, and Storage

Division with remainder connects these applications, but their moduli play different roles:

CycleModulusWhere it appears
Clock hours12Telling time; adding hours
Days of the week7Doomsday algorithm; calendars
Binary digits2Remainder gives a digit; quotient continues to higher places
Unsigned wraparound\(2^n\)Keeping only the lowest \(n\) bits
Hex / octal digits16 / 8Compact representation of binary

Congruence lets us calculate weekdays or wrapped values using remainders. Positional notation uses both quotient and remainder to preserve the full number.
